I have not spent nearly enough time to judge academia unbiasedly: my unconventional trajectory is exposed in my [CV](./assets/pdf/cv.pdf).
In fact, I believe I have been on this path for just enough time to see all its failings---and none of its virtues.
I will hence give my largely pessimistic review of a (very) early academic career; better resources by experienced and actually successful researchers may be found elsewhere.

_Your victories are shared; your defeats are not._
The relationship between an advisor and a PhD student is highly asymmetric: your advisor holds no skin in your game.
If you lose---if your papers are rejected, or you struggle to find an internship---, that it is entirely your problem.
If you don't lose---e.g., your works are well-regarded by the community, and your thesis is appreciated by your institution---, your advisor's role in your training will be greatly praised, and you might even play a key role in accelerating their path to tenure (should they still be in that path)!  
If they lose---e.g., if their funding stops, or the project they assigned to you was unfruitful---, you also lose.
That is to say: you share your victories, but mourn alone your defeats.  
In fact, after graduation, you can only hope that your advisor will assist you in your job search, and that they won't block you from requesting recommendation letters from your shared senior coauthors.
If they don't---truth be told---nobody will notice if your name is removed from the alumni section of their CVs.
As for the mathematically-inclined reader, this relationship may be better grasped through the following model: an advisor's payoff function is the average payoff function of his top-$K$ best students, for a (very) small $K$; unless you are a member of such select group, there is little-to-no reason to believe that your advisor will act in the best of your interest.

_Everyone declares to be solving the world's most pressing issues._
The academic road not only forces you to play a losing game, but also puts you in constant contact with people that will constantly emphasize their own greatness and the immense relevance of their job for society to justify---often to themselves---their primarily tax-payer funded projects.
From grounded but vague narratives, such as solving AI alignment or revolutionalizing the health or judicial sectors through technology, to bizarrely absurd declarations, such as meaningfully advancing the search for the cure to all diseases or the solution to the climate crisis, your colleagues and superiors will proudly state (primarily in prose, but also frequently in person) about their unmatched ambitions, which are second-to-none.
The main reason for this is that, while academia as an institution grew significantly since the 1940s across the world, resources for actually funding academic research have not followed the same trend.
An increasingly large pool of faculty now competes for a decreasing amount of publicly available funds, a setting that incentivizes excessive self-promotion and actively punishes honesty.

_Overqualified on things that don't matter; else, underqualified._
A common misconception I see online and among my non-academic acquaintances is that a PhD results in some kind of _overqualification_ that imposes hardships in finding a job thereafter.
I think that quite the opposite is true: a PhD leads to _overspecialization_ and _underqualification_ to most jobs.
A PhD in Computer Science is no better software engineer than a seasoned professional; and a PhD in Applied Mathematics is not more versed in data science than a fresh college graduate, even if only the former knows about Girsanov's theorem.
Indeed, it is a wildely acknowledged fact that a PhD is an underqualification even for a professorship; Peter Feibelman famously wrote an [entire book](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/587478) about this, and many PhDs continue as postdocs for a few years after graduation before landing their first (likely) permanent position---or giving up altogether.

_Modern LLMs have fucked everything up._
And now, with LLMs, everyone can be an academic!
The cost of writing a plausible-sounding manuscript, a task that once required Herculean effort and intensive training, has effectively vanished.
Those who have dedicated their lives to understanding and knowledge generation<d-footnote>Not my case, obviously.</d-footnote> now live in a world where each and everyone can, in the blink of an eye, achieve an amorphous version of the latter without regard for the former.
The leading venue in AI, for instance, published nearly ten thousands manuscripts only in 2026, and the scientific debate has notably shifted from the identification and solution of problems to grown and educated men squabbling on social media about who has the largest (number of published documents).

In conclusion, since abruptly defending my PhD thesis, when I relied upon the rare kindness of few but valuable friends---none of which were in academia---to remain away from unemployment in the months that followed my graduation, I have never felt more dreadful about the future.
Although my experiences are certainly non-unique and much of what I described is fairly obvious, I write this to communicate to the interested reader---if any---about the survivorship-biased perception regarding academic life suggested by their professors.
The pursuit of an academic career in my early 20s was a high-risk bet with limited upside and high probability of ruin.
